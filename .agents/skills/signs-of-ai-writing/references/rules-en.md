# English editorial review prompts

These examples are questions for an editor, not a list of forbidden words or a method for identifying authorship. Keep a phrase when it is precise, supported, and appropriate for the field.

## Claims and emphasis

- Does a sentence call something pivotal, transformative, or a testament? If so, identify the measurable change or source that supports the emphasis.
- Does the paragraph describe the subject's importance instead of explaining what happened, how a method works, or what a study found?
- Is praise such as *groundbreaking*, *world-leading*, or *state of the art* attributed to a source or supported by a stated comparison?
- Does a forecast say the subject is *poised* or *well-positioned*? Check the evidence, timeframe, and reason it belongs in the report.

## Attribution and evidence

- Replace vague attributions such as “experts say” only when the actual source can be identified. Do not invent a named expert to make the sentence sound specific.
- Distinguish the author's interpretation from a source's reported result.
- Check the original passage, table, or figure for each number and quotation. A matching title or DOI alone does not show that the source supports the claim.
- Use a direct quotation only when the exact wording matters; provide a page, section, or other locator where available.

## Sentence and paragraph clarity

- Check whether a trailing phrase such as “highlighting its importance” adds evidence or merely repeats the sentence.
- Check whether *not only … but also* expresses a real relationship. Keep it when it does.
- Remove throat-clearing only when it delays the point. Transitions such as *however* and *therefore* are normal when they state a real relationship.
- Break a long sentence when the reader must hold several claims, conditions, or citations in memory at once. Do not vary sentence length to imitate a presumed author.
- Keep lists when they help readers compare steps, criteria, items, or results.

## Formatting and artifact checks

- Remove chat greetings, internal citation tokens, unresolved template fields, and drafting notes when they are accidental residue.
- Keep legitimate dashes, quotation marks, lists, and headings. Punctuation alone is not an error.
- Confirm every in-text reference resolves to the intended bibliography item.
