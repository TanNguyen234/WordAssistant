# Limits of style-based review

Style observations cannot establish who wrote a passage. The same phrasing may be used by different authors, fields, translators, editors, templates, and language tools. No threshold of cliché counts, sentence lengths, punctuation, formatting, or grammatical regularity converts an editorial review into reliable authorship evidence.

## What a text scan can and cannot say

| Observation | Appropriate conclusion |
|---|---|
| A draft contains a generic transition or formal vocabulary | Ask whether it is clear and accurate in context. |
| A citation token or unfinished placeholder appears | Check whether it is accidental residue and repair it if needed. |
| A phrase sounds promotional or vague | Ask for a source or a more specific statement; do not infer authorship. |
| A passage has no citation | Review whether the claims require support and what sources are available. Lack of a citation does not identify the writer. |
| A passage is grammatical, repetitive, brief, or stylistically uniform | Treat that as a possible copyediting issue only when it affects the reader. |
| A source does not support a sentence | Correct, qualify, or remove the claim based on evidence, regardless of who drafted it. |

## Evidence about provenance

When provenance matters, use authorized records that directly address it: version history, drafts, research notes, source annotations, or the author's account of their process. Treat those records carefully and within the applicable policy. Do not substitute stylistic guesses for process evidence.

## Avoid false accusations

- Do not label a person, paragraph, or document “AI-written,” “human-written,” “plagiarized,” or “original” based on the linter or a phrase list.
- Do not use a deadline, publication date, or presumed technology cutoff as proof about a specific file unless the file's provenance has been verified.
- Do not rewrite a passage merely to change how a detector scores it. Edit for correctness, clarity, audience, and the author's stated goals.
- If an automated report is required by policy, explain the tool's limits and keep the result separate from a finding of misconduct.
